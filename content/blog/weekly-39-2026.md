-  Open Source, Communities and LLMs

    Jeff Atwood shared, on his blog, [this letter](https://blog.codinghorror.com/if-we-do-not-stop-to-help-each-other-what-do-we-become/) from a StackOverflow user about
    how the sense of community on the platform helped him and is something that's
    disappearing now.

    > So when I watch communities hollow out, replaced by something that gives
    > answers but doesn't give me a single thought... I get depressed. Because we're
    > losing something that was always there but I didn't notice. It's like losing
    > gravity.

    [This post](https://blog.glyph.im/2026/09/who-is-open-source-about.html) by Glyph on "Who is Open Source About" also touched on some adjacent
    topics, talking about Open Source and communities in the age of agentic
    programming.

    > As trying as it can be sometimes, we maintainers all do get something out of
    > open source, and it is good to be honest with your users — and with yourself —
    > exactly what you want to get out of it. In order to know whether the juice is
    > worth the squeeze, we must know both what the juice is, and what the squeeze
    > is.

    I do wonder how to do Open Source these days. Is Open Sourcing any of my tiny
    utilities of use any more? Is it for other people or just for AI to be trained
    on, for writing home-grown utitilies for others?

-  Enjoying programming / work

    I found [this Haskell discourse post](https://discourse.haskell.org/t/how-to-keep-enjoying-programming-in-a-world-of-llms/14705) on "How to keep enjoying programming in a
    world of LLMs" illuminating. Some of the suggestions about how to setup a
    workflow so you feel confident about the changes you make are stuff I've made
    note of, and plan to try.

    > It’s hard to build up trust in something that pretends to be much cleverer than
    > yourself. At the end, you’re responsible for the code you produce. Not your
    > machine. Blaming someone else for your code is something that bad managers and
    > colleagues do with their employees and coworkers, it’s ridiculous to do with a
    > machine. So use LLMs in a way that you can take responsibility for the results.
    > This only works if you make yourself an integral part of the process.

    On one morning, while I was practicing my cleans, I looked at a couple of the
    in-house trainers in our gym and had this epiphany -- isn't programming in the
    agentic world turning into something like their job?

    Most "trainers" in the local gyms here aren't doing much; most people coming to
    train know what they want to do, and how. There could be a handful of people
    who either take their help with specific exercises/weights, or even hire them
    to create "personalised" training plans and help them with it. In between this,
    they seem to be just doing their thing, watching from a distance what folks
    around are doing and probably not even registering much of it.

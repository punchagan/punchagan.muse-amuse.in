-  AI, math and computer science, society

    This week's big news was the Claude proof of 17M lines of Lean code of the
    Riemann Hypothesis being certified correct. I haven't read up on any of the
    discussion around it, and probably don't know enough Math to make sense of it.

    But, these couple of posts on this topic (or related ones) were thought
    provoking, even if on different ends of the spectrum.

    Anil Madhavapeddy's [post](https://anil.recoil.org/notes/software-too-complex-for-humans) on software development possibly being too complex for
    humans has a couple of interesting ideas -- rewriting everything ground-up
    based on formal specifications and using diversity as a defense mechanism.

    On the other hand, Rikab Gambhir asks "[Why are we even doing all this?](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/15/why-are-we-even-doing-all-this/)". In
    this satirical post he says:

    > By outsourcing the burdens of curiosity, creativity, and understanding, we can finally focus on what matters most: generating value, maximizing productivity, and accelerating progress towards the ultimate goal of science: a complete repo of potentially true facts in a Library of Babel that nobody will bother reading.

    I learnt about [Planck's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck%27s_principle#Formulation) this week.

    > A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it ...
    >
    > An important scientific innovation rarely makes its way by gradually winning over and converting its opponents: it rarely happens that Saul becomes Paul. What does happen is that its opponents gradually die out, and that the growing generation is familiarized with the ideas from the beginning: another instance of the fact that the future lies with the youth.

    This idea intuitively makes sense to me in the general society. I was surprised
    to hear Planck had this to say about the scientific community. Chaitu has
    ranted in the past about how biologists have a tough time coming to terms with
    the idea of theoretical biology and what it should entail. I wonder if this is
    what Planck was talking about.

    I wonder if this is something that could be extended to AI-assisted/boosted
    programming or other kinds of knowledge work too. Will the next generation of
    "programmers" or "knowledge workers" be better adapted to working this way, and
    push the envelope further than we ever could?
